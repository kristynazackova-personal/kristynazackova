Most of the hardest moments I've had as a PM didn't come from a bug, a deadline, or a difficult engineer. They came from being handed something vague — "we need to do something about retention," "the CEO wants AI in the product," "this customer is unhappy and we have to fix it" — and being expected to walk out of the room with a plan.

New PMs hate this. I hated this. The instinct is to look smart, nod, and start moving. That's how you end up four weeks deep in a roadmap nobody actually asked for.

There's a second flavor of this, too — the one that hits in your first three months at a new company, or when you get moved onto a product or domain you've never touched. The brief might be perfectly clear; *you* are the unknown variable. The team is moving, the project is in flight, and you're supposed to be making decisions about a thing you barely understand yet. That's its own kind of fog, and it deserves its own playbook.

Ambiguity isn't a bug in the job. It *is* the job. Senior PMs aren't the ones who avoid ambiguity — they're the ones who have a repeatable way of turning fog into a next step. Here's the process I teach, and the one I use myself when I get handed something I don't yet understand.

## 1. Name the ambiguity out loud

The first move isn't to solve the problem. It's to articulate, in plain language, *what is unclear*. Most ambiguity hides because nobody names it.

Try writing one sentence in the form: *"I'm not sure whether X or Y, because Z is undefined."*

Examples:

- "I'm not sure whether we're trying to grow new signups or retain existing users, because the goal in the brief says both."
- "I'm not sure whether 'fix this customer' means change the product or change the contract."
- "I'm not sure whether the CEO wants AI in the product because customers are asking, or because the board is asking."

If you can't write that sentence cleanly, you don't understand the ask well enough yet — and that's diagnostic. Half the work is in the wording.

## 2. Separate what you know from what you assume

Open a doc. Two columns. *Known* and *Assumed*. Be ruthless. Things you've personally seen data on go in *Known*. Things "everybody says" go in *Assumed*.

This sounds basic. It is. It is also the single highest-leverage habit a new PM can build. Most premature decisions are made by treating assumptions as facts because they were repeated by someone senior.

The point of this column isn't to *eliminate* assumptions — you can't. The point is to know which ones you're betting on, so when something breaks later you can trace it back to the bet, not the execution.

## 3. Map the stakeholders, not just the problem

Ambiguity is almost never one person's fault. It's usually that three people have three slightly different pictures in their heads and nobody has noticed.

List everyone who has a stake in this — sponsor, users, engineering lead, design, support, sales, legal if relevant. For each one, write two lines:

- **What do they think the problem is?**
- **What would "done" look like to them?**

You will be wrong about at least half of these. That's the value — now you have a list of cheap 15-minute conversations to have, each one a hypothesis to test. Most "ambiguous" projects are actually conflicts between stakeholder pictures that nobody has surfaced yet.

## 4. Write the strawman

This is the move that separates PMs who flail in ambiguity from PMs who navigate it: **propose something concrete, even if it's wrong.**

Write a one-page strawman: *here's what I think the problem is, here's who has it, here's what I'd do about it, here's what I'd measure, here's what I'd need*. Make it specific enough to disagree with.

People cannot react to vagueness. They can react to a proposal. A wrong strawman gets you a thousand times more signal than a "what do you think we should do?" email. You are not committing — you are *eliciting*. Say so out loud: *"This is a strawman to react to. I'm expecting to throw most of it away."*

Senior PMs do this constantly. It looks like confidence. It's actually a tool.

## 5. Triangulate before you commit

Before you build anything, get the same picture from at least three different angles. The classic triangle:

- **Users** — talk to at least five, ideally more. Real ones, not the proxy your sales team picked.
- **Data** — pull the numbers yourself, or sit with someone who can. Don't trust dashboards built for a different question than yours.
- **The team** — engineering and support see things nobody else does. Engineers know what's fragile. Support knows what's painful.

If all three angles point in the same direction, you have a real problem. If they don't, you have a more interesting problem — and you need to understand *why* they disagree before you decide whose picture is right.

A lot of new PMs skip this because the strawman felt good. Don't.

## 6. Time-box the discovery

Ambiguity expands to fill the calendar. If you give yourself "a few weeks to scope this out," you will spend a few weeks scoping it out, and at the end you will not have meaningfully more clarity than you had at the end of week one.

Pick a date. Say it out loud to your manager. *"By Friday the 14th, I'll have a strawman and a recommendation. It will be wrong in places. That's the point."*

This does two things. It forces you to make decisions instead of collecting more inputs. And it tells everyone around you that clarity is coming on a schedule — which is, very often, the actual thing your sponsor is anxious about.

Discovery that doesn't end isn't discovery. It's avoidance with a respectable name.

## 7. Make the decision visible

Once you've decided what you're doing — even provisionally — write it down where everyone can see it. Not in a slack thread that scrolls away. A doc, a one-pager, a decision log entry. Something durable.

Include:

- **What we're doing**, in one sentence.
- **Why** — the problem, in user or business terms.
- **What we're explicitly *not* doing**, and why. This section is the one that prevents 80% of future scope fights.
- **How we'll know it worked** — the metric, and the threshold.
- **What we're assuming** — the bets from step 2.
- **When we'll revisit** — a date, not a vibe.

A written decision is the artifact that converts ambiguity into alignment. It also gives future-you a paper trail when somebody six weeks from now asks "wait, why are we doing it this way?" Future-you will be grateful. I promise.

## When you're the one who's new

The seven steps above assume you know the terrain. Sometimes you don't — you've just started at a new company, or your manager moved you onto a product you've never touched, or the team is mid-flight on a project and you're expected to contribute *now*. The ambiguity isn't in the brief; it's in your own head. Different problem, related playbook.

The temptation is to disappear into reading-mode for a month and emerge with opinions. Don't. You learn a product by *touching* it — by writing, asking, summarizing, and being wrong out loud — not by lurking. Here's the rhythm I run, and the one I coach new joiners through.

### A 30/60/90 you can actually use

Pretty much every onboarding plan template online is useless because it's generic. The version that works is concrete and externally visible — your manager should be able to read it and nod.

- **Days 1–30 — Absorb and map.** Goal: understand the product, the people, and the metrics well enough to ask good questions. Don't ship decisions. Do ship *artifacts of understanding* — a written product map, a stakeholder list, a glossary of terms you didn't know on day one. These prove you're learning faster than you're being taught, which is what good managers actually want to see.
- **Days 31–60 — Contribute at the edges.** Goal: take real work, but pick scope where being wrong is cheap. Run the meeting nobody wants to run. Write the spec everyone's been meaning to write. Own the small-but-annoying bug that's been sitting in the backlog. You're earning trust and building product knowledge through the surface area of actual work.
- **Days 61–90 — Own something end-to-end.** Goal: take a real decision, with your name on it, on a scoped piece of the roadmap. It doesn't have to be big. It does have to be visibly *yours* — strawman, decision doc, metric, the full loop from section 7. This is the moment you stop being "the new PM" and start being "the PM for X."

Write this down in your first week. Share it with your manager. Ask them to push back. A manager who can't react to a concrete 30/60/90 is a manager you'll struggle with later — better to know now.

### The conversations to have in your first two weeks

Calendar discipline matters here. Aim for ~15 conversations in the first two weeks, 20–30 minutes each, with a specific question for each role. Generic "tell me about your job" coffee chats are a waste of everyone's time. Show up with one real question.

- **Your manager.** *"What does success look like for me at 30, 60, and 90 days — concretely? And what's the one thing that would make you regret hiring me?"* The second half of that question is the one that actually gets a useful answer.
- **Your tech lead / EM.** *"What's the part of this codebase or product that scares you? What should I not touch without asking?"* You'll learn more about the real architecture from this one question than from any doc.
- **Designer(s).** *"Where do you and product usually disagree, and how does it get resolved?"* You're learning the team's decision culture, not just the design process.
- **The PM who had this role before you** (if they exist). *"What did you wish you'd known on day one? What's still broken that you didn't get to fix?"* Pure gold. Pay for their coffee.
- **2–3 engineers on the team.** *"Walk me through what you're working on this sprint, and why it matters."* You're learning the product, the priorities, and how your team talks about its own work — all at once.
- **Customer support / success.** *"What are users complaining about this month that the product team doesn't seem to know about?"* The single highest-signal conversation in any onboarding. Support sees the product before anyone else does.
- **Sales (if applicable).** *"What do you wish the product did that it doesn't, and what deals did we lose because of it?"* Take the answers seriously, but with one filter: sales tells you about the deals you almost won, not the users you actually have.
- **One or two real users.** Yes, in your first two weeks. Even a 20-minute call. You will be tempted to wait until you "understand the product better." That instinct is exactly backwards — you understand the product better *because* you talked to users.

Take notes in the same doc every time. End of week two, re-read the whole thing. Patterns will jump out that you couldn't see in any single conversation.

### How to read the product without drowning

There's always more documentation than time. Triage ruthlessly. The order I use:

1. **Use the product yourself, every day, as a real user would.** If it's B2B and you can't get a real account, badger someone until you have one. This is non-negotiable. PMs who don't use their own product are a punchline for a reason.
2. **The last three months of release notes / changelogs.** Tells you what the team has actually been shipping, which is a much better signal than what the roadmap claims.
3. **The top dashboards the team looks at.** Not all of them. Ask "which three dashboards does this team actually check?" Then learn those three cold — what each metric means, how it's calculated, what counts as normal.
4. **The last 50 support tickets and the top 10 most-upvoted feature requests.** A two-hour exercise that will teach you more about user pain than a month of docs.
5. **Recordings of 3–5 recent sales or customer calls.** Whatever tool your team uses (Gong, Chorus, etc.), this is where you hear the product described in users' own words, including the parts that confuse them.
6. **Strategy docs and OKRs — last, not first.** I know that's counterintuitive. But strategy docs read very differently once you've talked to users and watched the product in action. Read them too early and you'll absorb a story you can't yet evaluate.

Set a hard cap: no more than two hours a day on async reading in your first month. The rest goes to conversations and using the product. Reading scales infinitely. Your trust-building window does not.

### Contributing to an in-flight project without slowing it down

This is the scenario that scares new PMs most: the team is mid-execution on something complicated, you don't fully understand it, and you're now the PM. You have two bad options and one good one.

The bad options: pretend to understand and make decisions anyway (you'll be wrong, visibly), or hide and let the team run itself until you "feel ready" (you'll never feel ready, and they'll stop expecting anything from you).

The good option: **take the jobs nobody else wants to do, that force you to comprehend the project in order to do them well.** Specifically:

- **Write the meeting summaries.** Nobody is doing this. It's tedious. It also forces you to follow every thread, name every open question, and surface every decision — which is the *exact* mental model you need. After three weeks of this, you'll know the project better than half the people in the room.
- **Own the open-questions list.** Every in-flight project has a pile of unresolved questions floating between Slack threads. Collect them in one doc. Tag each one with an owner and a date. Chase them. This is pure PM work, it's visibly useful from day one, and it teaches you the project's real shape.
- **Run the cross-functional sync.** The recurring meeting that nobody quite owns? Take it. Set a tight agenda, run it to time, send notes after. Three weeks in, you'll be the connective tissue of the project, which is the PM's actual job description.
- **Don't override decisions you don't yet understand.** When you see a decision that looks wrong, your first move is to ask *why* it was made — in a 1:1, not in a meeting. Nine times out of ten there's a reason you weren't aware of. The tenth time, you've earned the right to push back, because you bothered to understand the nine.

Your goal in the first month on an in-flight project is not to change its direction. It's to become the person the team trusts to *protect* its direction. Direction-changing comes later, when you have the standing for it.

### "I don't know yet" vs "nobody knows"

The most important skill in your first 90 days is telling these two apart. They look identical from the outside and have completely opposite responses.

*"I don't know yet"* means the answer exists, in someone's head or in a doc, and you haven't found it. Response: keep asking, keep reading, write down what you find. Cheap to resolve.

*"Nobody knows"* means there is no answer — the question hasn't actually been thought through by anyone. Response: this is yours to figure out, and naming it as a real open question (in writing, with your name next to it) is one of the highest-leverage things a new PM can do. The team will be quietly grateful that someone finally said it out loud.

New PMs default-assume everything is in the first bucket — that they're the only one who doesn't know. They're often wrong. The fastest way to look senior in your first 90 days is to surface a "nobody knows" that the whole team had been walking around for six months, name it cleanly, and propose a way to resolve it. That's the move.

## Phrases that pull clarity toward you

The right question, asked plainly, does more than a week of analysis. A few that I use constantly:

- **"What would change if we knew the answer to this?"** — Forces the room to admit when a question doesn't actually matter, vs. when it's a real fork in the road.
- **"What does done look like?"** — Sounds basic. Catches people every time. If three people give three different answers, you've found the ambiguity.
- **"What are we explicitly *not* solving here?"** — Defining the negative space is sometimes the entire job.
- **"If you had to pick one metric to judge this by, which one?"** — Strips out the comfortable "and also" goals that turn projects into mush.
- **"Who's going to be upset if we ship this?"** — Surfaces the political constraints nobody put in the brief.
- **"What's the worst version of this that would still be worth doing?"** — Floor-finding. Often reveals that the scope you thought was non-negotiable actually is.

You don't need clever questions. You need plain ones, asked without flinching.

## Traps that swallow new PMs

A short list of the patterns I see most often. I've fallen into all of them.

**Mistaking activity for progress.** Booking ten meetings doesn't reduce ambiguity. Writing things down does. If you finish a week and can't point to a doc that's clearer than it was on Monday, the week didn't move.

**Waiting for permission to think.** Nobody is coming to hand you a clean problem statement. Your manager doesn't have one either. Write the strawman. Send it. The worst case is you get corrected, which is also the best case.

**Mirroring the loudest voice in the room.** When you don't have your own picture yet, the picture of whoever spoke last feels true. Don't decide in the meeting. Decide on paper, the next morning, after you've drafted the strawman alone.

**Confusing the symptom with the problem.** "Our NPS is down" is a symptom. The problem might be onboarding, pricing, a competitor, or a single broken email. New PMs ship fixes for symptoms and wonder why the metric doesn't move.

**Treating ambiguity as a sign that you're failing.** It isn't. It's a sign that you've been trusted with something that hasn't been solved yet. The discomfort doesn't go away with seniority — you just get faster at moving through it.

## Closing

Ambiguity is the texture of the work. Nobody hands a PM a clean problem; if it were clean, they wouldn't need a PM. The skill isn't avoiding the fog — it's having a process you trust to walk through it.

Name the ambiguity. Sort known from assumed. Map the people. Write the strawman. Triangulate. Time-box. Make the decision visible. Repeat. And when *you're* the unknown variable — new job, new product, new domain — work the same way on yourself: use the product, write the artifacts of understanding, take the small jobs that force comprehension, and call out "nobody knows" when you spot it.

You will still feel uncertain. That's not a defect in the process. That's the process working — uncertainty is now bounded, named, and on a schedule.
